Generated by `python3 scripts/explore_cmu_drone.py` from the untouched raw copy in `data/raw/cmu_drone/`.
After cloning, unzip the main data file first: `gunzip -k data/raw/cmu_drone/flights.csv.gz` (it is stored compressed because GitHub rejects files over 100 MB).

## Structure

- `flights.csv`: 257,896 rows × 28 columns. No missing values and no duplicate rows. Sampled at about 5 Hz (median step 0.12 s).
- Fields: `time` (s since takeoff), `wind_speed` (m/s) and `wind_angle` (deg) from an onboard anemometer, `battery_voltage` (V), `battery_current` (A), `position_x/y` (lon/lat deg), `position_z` (m above sea level), orientation quaternion, velocity, angular velocity, linear acceleration, plus per-flight `speed` (m/s), `payload` (g), `altitude` (m), `date`, `time_day` and `route`.
- There are **209 recordings**. 196 are cruise flights (routes R1–R7, of which 182 are R1). The other 13 are ancillary: 9 ground tests (A1–A3) and 4 hovers (H).
- Planned speeds for cruise flights are 4/6/8/10/12 m/s, roughly balanced at 36–43 flights each. Payloads are 0/250/500 g (62–67 flights each) plus a single 750 g flight. Altitudes are 25/50/75/100 m, plus two R7 flights flown at varying altitude (`"25-50-100-25"`).

## Timeline

- Flights span **2019-04-07 to 2019-10-24** on **18 flight days**. Most fall in June–July (63 and 97 flights). There are only 6 in April and 5–6 each in September and October.
- Flight IDs are mostly chronological but not strictly. Within a day, a few IDs are out of time order (e.g. flight 109 at 10:39 comes before 112 at 10:06). Sort by `date` + `time_day`, not by ID.
- This is a real timeline across seasons, but it is a parameter-sweep experiment, not an operational history. Any drift scenario built on it must be labeled as a simulated progression.

## Per-flight energy

Energy = ∫ V·I dt (trapezoid rule) over the whole recording, from takeoff to landing.

| Cruise flights (n=196) | Value |
|---|---|
| Energy (Wh) | median 20.7, IQR 17.6–24.5, range 13.0–37.7 |
| Duration (s) | median 184, range 106–428 |
| Ground distance (m) | median 458; flight 272 flew about 828 m |

Mean energy by planned setting (one factor at a time, not controlled for the others):

- **Payload:** 0 g → 19.2 Wh, 250 g → 21.4, 500 g → 23.1 (750 g: 25.8, n=1)
- **Speed:** 4 m/s → 27.0 Wh, 6 → 22.1, 8 → 19.8, 10 → 18.5, 12 → 18.5. Slow flights take longer and hover-like power dominates.
- **Altitude:** 25 m → 17.6 Wh, 50 → 20.4, 75 → 22.6, 100 → 25.2. Climbing costs energy and lengthens the flight.

Hover recordings draw about 360–470 W, while the ground tests draw 0–35 W.

## Data issues observed in the source (not introduced by us)

- **Current sensor offset:** idle current sits around −0.1 A. This yields slightly negative energy for A1 ground tests, but is negligible for flights.
- **Timing gaps:** flights 269 (2.1 s), 278 (5.3 s) and 279 (4.7 s) contain gaps longer than 2 s.
- **Likely time-of-day typo:** flight 150 logged at "3:10" on 2019-07-03 sits between morning flights and is probably 15:10 or a data-entry error.
- **Distance outlier:** flight 272 (R6, 4 m/s, 250 g) is the longest and most energy-hungry flight. It flew a longer path, so it is not a sensor error.
- **README mismatch:** the source README gives the collection range as 20190704–20191024, but the data starts on 2019-04-07.

## Implications for the experiment

- With only 196 independent flights, use grouped/whole-flight splits, repeated seeds and bootstrap confidence intervals. Don't treat sensor rows as samples.
- Energy is strongly explained by planned speed, payload and altitude. A pre-flight energy predictor is a natural "decision" task, for example go/no-go or reserve sizing.
- Wind and battery-voltage readings are the obvious fields to degrade: stale (frozen or last-value-carried-forward) and low-quality (bias or noise from a high-volume cheap sensor feed).
